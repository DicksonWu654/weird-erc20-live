Every token says ERC-20 on the label.

That is supposed to mean they all work the same way, so apps can treat them the same. I opened up {{L:n_universe}} tokens on {{L:n_chains}} chains, including the {{L:n_main}} biggest by market cap, and tested what they actually do. Then I simulated buying and selling {{L:v2_pooled_n}} brand-new ones an hour after launch. They don’t work the same.

Built with a swarm of AI agents, so there will be mistakes. Every number links to its evidence. Corrections welcome.

What the biggest tokens actually do

Things the standard does not promise. Each one can break an app or surprise a holder.

USDT’s transfer() says nothing back.

The standard says transfer() should answer true when it works. USDT on Ethereum answers nothing.

What breaks An app written by the book rejects every USDT transfer on Ethereum. It is rare, with {{T:prevalence_table|row=Missing return values|col=Ranked: yes / measured (rate)|part=num}} of {{T:prevalence_table|row=Missing return values|col=Ranked: yes / measured (rate)|part=den}} measured top tokens doing it, but the biggest stablecoin is one of them.

That is why DeFi code wraps token calls in helpers like SafeERC20 from OpenZeppelin.

Some tokens fail the other way. All of that is about one chain at a time. The same ticker on another chain can be a different token.

Same ticker, different token.

Remember USDT and its decimals: one coin, a trillion apart on two chains. That is not a one-off. The same ticker on another chain is usually a separate contract, deployed by someone else, with its own rules. What a token does on one chain tells you little about the same ticker elsewhere.

PayPal’s PYUSD shows it. I simulated sending each copy a transfer to the Tornado Cash router, an address the US sanctions authority delisted on {{P:2025-03-21}}. On Ethereum and Arbitrum the token refuses. On Polygon it lets the transfer through. It is not in our tested set on the other three chains.

Why? The test sends to the same router address on every chain, so a refusal means that copy keeps its own list with the address on it. What the data does show is that the copies run {{K:distinct|paypal-usd}} different pieces of code. Polygon’s is a small {{K:size|polygon|0x99aF3EeA856556646C98c8B9b2548Fe815240750}}-byte shell that {{K:count|polygon|0x99aF3EeA856556646C98c8B9b2548Fe815240750}} tokens in our set use, PYUSD one of them, so it was built differently from the other two. A refusal can also come from a list nobody updated after the delisting. Exactly why Polygon’s copy lets the transfer through, I do not know.

What breaks An app that assumes one chain’s behaviour carries over to another gets it wrong in either direction.

UNI’s quirk never left Ethereum.

Earlier, UNI on Ethereum capped an “unlimited” approval. On the other four chains it does not. The copies are again different code, and here the data says a bit more. On Arbitrum, UNI runs one {{K:size|arbitrum|0xFa7F8980b0f1E64A2062791cc3b0871572f1F7f0}}-byte contract shared by {{K:count|arbitrum|0xFa7F8980b0f1E64A2062791cc3b0871572f1F7f0}} tokens in our set, UNI and LINK among them. On Polygon it is another shared contract, {{K:size|polygon|0xb33EaAd8d922B1083446DC23f610c2567fB5180f}} bytes.

A template shared by many unrelated tokens is what bridge-issued tokens tend to look like, and it would explain why they carry none of the original’s quirks. On Polygon our data marks UNI as a bridged copy, along with {{K:bridged|polygon|0xb33EaAd8d922B1083446DC23f610c2567fB5180f}} of the {{K:others|polygon|0xb33EaAd8d922B1083446DC23f610c2567fB5180f}} other tokens that share its code. On Arbitrum the pattern looks the same, but I have not traced those copies to a bridge.

Someone decides which of those lists and switches exist. Who holds them?

Then the switches someone else holds.

Some tokens ship with a pause button or a freeze list, and you are not the one holding it. Tokenized stocks and funds are the extreme case: {{B:blocklist}} of the {{B:total}} among the top tokens have a blocklist.

BUIDL, a tokenized fund, goes further: it rejects transfers to wallets it has not approved {{XS:ethereum|0x7712c34205737192402172409a8f7ccef8aa2aec|permissioned_transfer}}. PEPE still has a blocklist in its code, but its owner gave up the role {{XS:ethereum|0x6982508145454ce325ddbe47a25d4ec3d2311933|owner_renounced}}. Whether anyone can still use the list, the code alone does not say.

What breaks A paused token fails every transfer. A blocked address keeps its balance but can’t send it.

Example, USDC on Ethereum has blocklist functions: {{X:ethereum|0xa0b86991c6218b36c1d19d4a2e9eb0ce3606eb48|blocklist_capability}}.

A switch can stop your tokens. A key can rewrite them.

Some household names answer to one key.

The upgrade call is the proof. On Ethereum I replayed it at a pinned block: from the one wallet that holds the key it succeeds, from anyone else it reverts. USDC, PYUSD, cbBTC and BlackRock’s BUIDL all behave this way.

About four in ten of the top tokens can have their code replaced: {{N:upgradeable.yes}} of the {{N:upgradeable.measured}} we could read. For {{L:main_upgrade_eoa_n_yes}}, a single wallet key can do it, with no multisig and no timelock in the way. For {{N:multisig_no_timelock}} more it takes a multisig, still with no timelock, so there is no delay and no warning before a change. Tokenized stocks and funds are a big part of it: {{B:upgradeable}} of the {{B:total}} can be upgraded.

What breaks Whoever holds the key can change what the token does for every holder at once. On-chain you can see which account holds the key, not how it is kept.

Part two: brand-new tokens

Now the newest tokens.

Those are the biggest, best-known tokens on six chains. The newest tokens get far less scrutiny, so I went looking at them next.

Buy it, wait an hour, try to sell.

Anyone can launch a token in minutes, and it can look fine on day one. So I took brand-new Ethereum tokens, found each one’s first trading pool, and waited about an hour ({{P:300 blocks}}). Then I simulated a test buy of {{P:0.01 ETH}} and a sale in the next block. Nothing was ever sent on-chain: every test is replayed against saved chain state.

{{L:v2_pooled_n}} tokens that got a pool went through this, sampled by the month they were created.

One last thing: my own measuring almost fooled me.

The measurement that almost fooled me.

Counting drained pools needs a line: a pool counts as live only above {{P:0.01 ETH}}. My first version used a tiny line, and it showed a strange blip. On {{P:2025-12-27}}, at Ethereum block {{L:fee_activation_block}}, Uniswap V2 switched on its protocol fee. After that, when a provider pulled out of a pool, the protocol could be left holding a sliver of shares, and a small balance stayed behind.

That leftover could sit just above my tiny line, so a drained pool looked alive again. The balance was real, but it was only the protocol’s sliver plus the small amount every V2 pool locks away forever. The provider had left, and nothing had come back. I reproduced the fee formula on {{L:fee_formula_checks}} saved receipts to be sure, then moved the line to {{P:0.01 ETH}} and applied it the same way on every venue.

What breaks A line set too low counts a drained pool as alive, and new launches look healthier than they are.

How it was measured

The biggest tokens

  • Tested set: the top CoinGecko rank bands on each chain plus {{L:n_extras}} extras, {{L:n_universe}} deployments in all. Rates use the {{L:n_main}} ranked deployments; extras are shown separately. Only rank bands are published, never market data.
  • Every test is a read-only call or a local simulation against archive state; nothing is broadcast. Each cell keeps the raw request and response pointer, block, retrieval time and a sha256 of the saved response.
  • “Yes” answers the column’s question. Capability columns record what the code exposes; how or whether anyone uses it is outside what calls can show.
  • “Could not test” always carries a reason (no balance slot, transfers blocked, timeouts). It counts as neither yes nor no.
  • An upgrade path that ends at an externally owned account describes the on-chain account type. How that key is held (a person, MPC, a hardware module) is invisible on-chain.
  • Tornado Cash router rejections can come from a stale or independent policy list. The tested router is the one OFAC delisted on {{P:2025-03-21}}.
  • {{B:total}} ranked deployments are tokenized stocks and funds, found by issuer name or listing. They lift the upgrade and blocklist shares, so the catalogue shows each rate with and without them.
  • Named issuers appear only next to a reproduced on-chain check, with the deployment and block stated.

The new launches

  • A probability sample of new Ethereum tokens by creation month; each token’s earliest pool with liquidity.
  • The test buy is {{P:0.01 ETH}}, simulated {{P:300 blocks}} after first liquidity, with the sale in the following block.
  • A pool counts as live when its real quote liquidity at that moment is at least {{P:0.01 ETH}}, on every venue (V1/V2, V3, V4).
  • A trap means the sale fails or the tax is {{P:10% or more}}. “Could not buy”, unresolved routes and below-floor pools are reported as their own categories.
  • Brackets are approximate {{P:95%}} sampling intervals. They exclude classification error.
  • Any rate resting on an effective sample {{P:below 30}} is withheld and shown as “Not reported”.
  • Drained pools are a description of liquidity, with no attribution to whoever withdrew it.
